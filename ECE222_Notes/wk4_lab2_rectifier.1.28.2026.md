###### Forward Voltage Drop: 
The voltage amplitude across the $1k\ohm$ resistor is $4.159V$
This means the forward voltage drop is $V{source}_m - V_{R} = 5.000 - 4.159 = 0.841V$
###### Peak Current:
The peak current through the diode should be the same everywhere in the circuit as it is in the resistor, since it is a series circuit, $\frac{4.159V}{1000\ohm} = 4.159mA$
###### Explanation:
The diode is allowing the positive voltage to flow through, but clipping the negative voltage.
###### Predictions for 20$V_{pp} @ 60Hz$
At $20V_{pp}$, the peak voltage across the resistor should be around $10-0.841 = 9.359V$, and experience similar clipping behavior.

###### Data:

| Capacitor          | $V_{out}\ min$ | $V_{out}\ max$ | $t\ v_{max}\to v_{min}$ | $\tau = RC$                                        |
| ------------------ | -------------- | -------------- | ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| $10\micro F$       | $2.350V$       | $8.810V$       | $12.916ms$              | $1000\times 10\times 10^{-6} = 10\times 10^{-3}$   |
| $47\micro F$       | $5.750V$       | $7.824V$       | $12.916ms$              | $1000\times 47\times 10^{-6} = 47\times 10^{-3}$   |
| $100\micro F$      | $6.464V$       | $7.348V$       | $12.916ms$              | $1000\times 1\times 10^{-4} = 1\times 10^{-1}$     |
| $470 \micro F$     | $6.804V$       | $7.076V$       | $12.916ms$              | $1000\times 4.7\times 10^{-4} = 4.7\times 10^{-1}$ |
| $1000\micro F$<br> | $6.872V$       | $7.008V$       | $12.916ms$              | $1000\times 1\times 10^{-3} = 1$                   |
###### Explanation:
The diode is clipping the AC wave, preventing any current from flowing during the negative portion of the oscillation.

When the source voltage falls below the voltage of the capacitor at the positive terminal, it begins to supply positive voltage to the circuit, but discharges. So the voltage drops but not as suddenly as if there were no capacitor, as seen in circuit 1.

As the capacitance increases, the time constant of the RC circuit increases.

Since the frequency of the source voltage never changes, the time $V_{source}$ is rising is $\frac{1}{2}$ the period of the source voltage sine wave, $1.6\times 10^{-2}s$, when $\tau$ is relatively small:
$$\Large e^{\frac{-t}{\tau}} = e^{-\frac{16\times 10^{-3}}{10\times 10^{-3}}} = e^{-1.6}$$
We can see from the results, that the capacitor discharges quite a bit, maybe not fully as it would if $e^{-5}$ but quite a bit, hence a larger "ripple" magnitude.

For a large capacitor, $\tau$ is much larger, equal to $1$ actually for the $1000\micro F$ capacitor. 
In this case, $e^{-16\times 10^3}$ is considerably less than $e^{-5}$ so the capacitor's positive voltage discharges a lot slower, leading to a more stable, better rectified constant, nearly DC signal at around $7.5$ to $8V$.

I'd predict that the waveform at $V_{out}$ with the $47\micro F$ cap will be a similar sawtooth with approximately 1/10th the magnitude of the $470\micro F$ cap.

I didn't have a $1\ohm$ resistor, and used a $10\ohm$ resistor instead. This resistor is in series with the diode and therefore sees the same current as it.

The voltage drop across the $10\ohm$ resistor is divided by $10$ to give the current through the diode and the resistor. We'd expect around $0.841V$ to drop across the diode (forward voltage), leaving about $9.159V$ to drop across the $10$ and $1k\ohm$ resistors in series.

The voltage drop across the $10\ohm$ should be $9.159 \times \frac{10}{1010} = 0.0907V$, and then the current can be estimated as $\frac{0.0907V}{10\ohm} = 9.07\times 10^{-3}A = 9.07mA$ 

This needs to be done, because after the diode, the voltage and current are being clipped by the diode properties (i.e there is no negative half of the wave). Because of this neither a DC or AC meter could accurately measure the current or voltage, and the scope does not yield an accurate measurement in current probe mode.

We must put a small resistor in and measure the voltage, ideal if the resistor is $1\ohm$ because the voltage drop across it will also be the current.
($\frac{V_{measured}}{1\ohm} = V_{measured} = i_{diode}$).

On the scope, a $0.1\times$ attenuation was applied, to offset the fact that the resistor used was $10\ohm$ not $1\ohm$. However, something is still not right, maybe the wrong resistor (mistakenly a $1k\ohm$) was used because the current is still off by about 2 order of magnitude.
