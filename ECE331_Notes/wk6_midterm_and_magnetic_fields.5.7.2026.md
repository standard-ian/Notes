#### Capacitance Example Continued
Recall that for 2 parallel and equal/opposite charged sheets, when they are a distance apart that is relatively small, the F field is from the positive to the negative plate, and the external field cancels.

$$\vec{E} = -\nabla V = E_y^+ + E_y^{-} = \frac{\rho_s}{2\epsilon_0} - \frac{\rho_s}{2\epsilon_0}$$
$$\rho_s = \frac{C}{m^2} = \frac{Q}{A_c}$$
$$\vec{E} = \hat{y}E_y = -\frac{\rho_s}{\epsilon_0}\hat{y}\tag{4} = -\frac{Q}{A_c\epsilon_0}\hat{y}$$
$$V = -\int_0^{d}\vec{E}\ d\vec{l}\tag{5}$$
$$\vec{E} = E_y\hat{y} d\vec{l} = dy\hat{y}\tag{6}$$
Subbing $(4)$ and $(6)$ into $(5)$:
$$V=-\int_0^d-\left(\frac{Q}{A_c\epsilon_0}\right)\hat{y}\ \cdot \hat{y}\ dy$$
$$V = \frac{Qd}{A_c\epsilon_0}$$
or, $C = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{A_c\epsilon_0}{d}$ 
For $y$ in Air
$A_c = 0.01m$
$d = 1\times 10^{-6} m (0.1mm)$
$$C = \frac{0.01^2\epsilon_0}{1\times 10^{-6}} = 8.854\times 10^{-12}F$$
When there is a dielectric material $\epsilon_r$
$$C = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{\epsilon_0\epsilon_rA}{d}$$
#### Example: Cylindrical Capacitor
Of length $L$ and inner conductor $a$ with positive charge charge and outer conductor $b$ with a negative charge. 

There is a non-constant $\vec{E}$ pointing radially out from the surface of the center conductor $a$. 

###### For $a < r < b$
$$\oint_{s_l} \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{s} = \frac{Q_{enc}}{\epsilon_0}$$
There will be some fringing effects on the ends of the capacitor, but we will imagine a small diameter relative to $L$, allowing us to disregard these effects. 

So $\vec{E}$ is radially outward all over the surface we'll proceed as if there is no $E_z$ component if the field.

$$\vec{E} = E_r\hat{r}$$
To evaluate the surface of the cylinder:
$$\int_0^L\int_0^{2\pi}E_r r\ \ d\theta\ dz = \frac{Q_{enc}}{\epsilon_0}$$
$$E_r2\pi rL = \frac{Q_{enc}}{\epsilon_0}$$
Enclosed in the Gaussian cylinder within the gab between the conductors, is the conductor $a$
$$E_r = \frac{Q}{2\pi rL\epsilon_0}$$
Now we need the potential in order to determine capacitance
$$V_{ab} = -\int_b^a\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l};\ \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l} = E_r\hat{r}\cdot \hat{r}\ dr$$
$$V_{ab} = -\int_b^a\frac{Q\hat{r}}{2\pi rL\epsilon_0}dr = \frac{-Q}{2\pi L \epsilon_0\epsilon_r}(\ln[a]-\ln[b])$$
$$= \frac{Q}{2\pi L\epsilon}\ln\left[\frac{b}{a}\right]$$
Therefore, the capacitance is:
$$\boxed{C = \frac{Q}{V_{ab}} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon L}{\ln\left[\frac{b}{a}\right]}}$$
#### 2.14 Electrostatic Energy
Energy in an electric field can be shown to be given by the integral of the permittivity times the magnitude of the field over some volume.

$$W_T = \frac{1}{2}\int_{V_c}\epsilon|E|^2\ dv$$
or
$$W_T = \frac{1}{2}\int_V\frac{|D|^2}{\epsilon}\ dv\ \ [J]$$
Note: $W_{Td} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{|D|^2}{\epsilon} = \frac{1}{2}\epsilon|E|^2$ is energy density, $\frac{J}{m^3}$ 

$$|E|^2 = \vec{E}\cdot \vec{E};\ \ \epsilon = \epsilon_0\epsilon_r$$
But $\vec{D} = \epsilon\vec{E}$

Consider the capacitor with plates of some area $A$ and some distance of separation $L$.

Neglecting fringing:
$$V = \int_0^d \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l} = \int_0^dE_y\ dy\Longrightarrow E_y = \frac{V}{L}$$
Assume $\epsilon$ is constant:
$$W_T = \frac{1}{2}\epsilon\int_{V_l} |E|^2\ dV_c$$
Volume $V_c = Ad$
$$W_T = \int_{V_l} \frac{V
^2}{L^2}dV_c = \frac{\epsilon}{2}\frac{V^2}{L^2} LA$$
$$W_T = \frac{1}{2}V^2\left(\frac{A\epsilon}{L}\right)$$
$$W_T = \frac{1}{2}VC = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C}$$
#### 3.0 Steady Electric Current
The goal is to break down the following equation.
**Equation of Continuity**
$$\boxed{\nabla \cdot \vec{J} = -\frac{\partial \rho_v}{\partial t}\tag{20}}$$
In a metal, current is generated by the motion of electrons between the atoms. 

This is called **conduction current** and it generates an electric field.

**Convection current** results from the motion of electrons in a vacuum or when there is no electric field, for example, solar wind (does not obey Ohm's law).

#### 3.1 Convection Current
Consider a given incremental volume $dV$ contains charge density $\rho_V$.

$$dQ = \rho_V\ dV\tag{1}$$
Imagine a cylinder, with a bunch of charge inside floating around in a vacuum. It has a tiny length $dl$. It has a circular face (end) with surface area $ds$ and a vector normal to this end surface $\hat{n}$

$$dV = ds\ dl \tag{2}$$
So, substituting $(2)$ into $(1)$:
$$dQ = \rho_V\ dsdl\tag{3}$$
If charges are moved (by, say wind) in the direction $dl$ (the $z$ direction). Then, the charges are moving at a velocity $V_z$:
$$dl = V_z\ dt\tag{4}$$
So, we can substitute $(4)$ into $(3)$ which gives
$$dQ = \rho_V\ dsV_z\ dt$$
Or:
$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = dI = \rho_V\ ds V_z \left[\frac{C}{s} = A\right]\tag{5}$$
If charges are not moving normal to the end surface $ds$, to define the amount of charge going through the end surfaces, we need to dot the charge density times the volume vector with the normal:
$$dI = \rho_V\vec{V}\cdot \hat{n}\ ds$$
We can now define current density as:
$$J = \rho_V\vec{V}\left[\frac{A}{m^2}\right] \tag{7}$$
Charge density divided by the velocity of the charges.
$$\frac{C}{m^3}\frac{m}{s} = \frac{C}{s}\frac{1}{m^2} = \frac{A}{m^2}$$
Substituting $(7$) back into $(6)$:
$$dI = \vec{J}\cdot \hat{n}\ ds$$
So the current density can be written:
$$\frac{dI}{ds} = \vec{J}\cdot \hat{n}\tag{8}$$
The current density that is flowing out **normal** to the "end" surface of $dl$.

If we evaluate this over the surface $ds$, we get the definition of current with respect to the current density.

Evaluating $\vec{J}$ (current density) over surface:
$$I = \int_S \vec{J}\cdot \hat{n}\ ds_0\tag{9}$$
See 7.1, 7.2 in Ida 4th Ed. for more examples.

#### 3.2 Conduction Current
Current flow through a conductor is described by:
$$\vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E}\tag{10}$$
Where $\sigma = \text{conductivity}$ and $\frac{1}{\sigma} =\text{resistivity}$
$$\frac{J}{E} = \frac{A}{m^2}\frac{m}{V} = \frac{A}{mV} = \frac{S}{m}$$

#### 3.4 Equation of Continuity
A fundamental postulate is that the electric charge cannot be created or destroyed. 

The current leaving a region is the total outward flux of the current density vector through a surface $s$.

Recalling the following (equation $(9)$)
$$I = \int_S \vec{J}\cdot \hat{n}\ ds_0$$
$$\int_S\vec{J}\cdot d\vec{s} = -\frac{dQ}{dt}\tag{18}$$
Negative sign since $I$ (current) flows **out** of the surface. The charge will **decrease** at a rate equal to the current.

$$-\frac{dQ}{dt} = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_V\rho_VdV_0 \tag{19}$$
Where $\int_V\rho_V dV_0 = Q$ 
Substituting $(19)$ into $(18)$:
$$\int_S \vec{J}\cdot d\vec{s} = \frac{d}{dt}\int\rho_V\ dV_0$$
Using divergence theorem
$$\int_V\nabla \vec{J}\ dV = \frac{d}{dt}\int_V\rho_V\ dV_0$$
or:
$$\nabla \cdot \vec{J} = -\frac{\partial\rho_V}{\partial t}\tag{20}$$
The equation of continuity!
